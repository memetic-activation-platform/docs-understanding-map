    Technology as if Life Mattered¶

    Reimagining the Technological Substrate of Civilization

    Why These Essays Exist¶

    This essay is part two of a longer reflection that began with Because of its length, it will be published here in several installments.

    The first essay attempted to name the deeper pattern of our situation: the unraveling of many of the systems that have shaped the modern world, and the possibility that we are living through a civilizational transition rather than a collection of unrelated crises.

    This essay begins from that premise.

    Because this essay discusses technology, it risks being misunderstood. So I want to make three things clear from the outset.

    1. If you’re tempted to skip this essay because you don’t consider yourself “technical,” I hope you’ll stay with me.

    The questions explored here are not primarily technical, they are civilizational. The systems through which societies coordinate—economic, political, cultural, and technological—shape how we live together, and they are deeply interdependent. The principles guiding those systems belong to all of us.

    2. This essay is NOT written from the belief that technology will solve the challenges we face.

    Many of the most important responses to this moment require no technological mediation at all. They arise through direct relationship: people restoring land, strengthening communities, and learning again how to live within the limits of living systems. Those processes are fundamentally relational. Technology cannot replace them. In many contexts technological interventions actually make things worse.

    3. Yet technology still matters — because it shapes how societies coordinate at scale.

    Technology is never truly neutral. Every tool carries assumptions about how the world should work and quietly reshapes the systems around it.

    Some technologies primarily extend human capability. Others begin to shape how societies organize themselves. It is this second category—technologies that structure coordination, incentives, and power at scale—that this essay is primarily concerned with.

    When embedded within an economic system organized around extraction, competition, and growth, such technologies reinforce and accelerate existing patterns — concentrating power, deepening asymmetries, and intensifying the forces driving collapse.

    Part of the purpose of this essay is therefore diagnostic: to help us see how the technologies surrounding us quietly encode and reinforce the memes of material culture. Until we can see those patterns, we cannot free ourselves from them.

    But technology does not have to serve those purposes. Its proper role is far more profound: to serve life.

    A Long Journey to This Moment¶

    In 2015, I heard Drew Dellinger deliver a reading of his poem . His measured delivery of the line “what did you do, once... you... knew,” broke me. In that moment, my heartbreak over the direction civilization was headed (and my complicity in that story), compelled me to make a decision: to devote myself to answering that question.

    I couldn’t plead ignorance. By that time, I had become convinced that the issues we face are global, systemic and deeply rooted in a worldview that has outgrown its usefulness. Global climate change, ocean acidification, species extinction, polarization of wealth, the rise of authoritarianism, etc. are all just symptoms. The deeper problem lies in our civilizational systems and the assumptions that produced them.

    I had also spent more than thirty years designing and implementing complex systems as a software developer and architect, platform architect, and enterprise architect – largely within Fortune 500 companies. I felt called to bring that experience to the task of responding to the poem’s provocative question, not because I believed technology itself was the answer, but because that experience happened to be what I had to offer.

    In 2016, the vision for what I call the Memetic Activation Platform (MAP) came into view over the course of several months. During that period, the core architecture and intent of the system took shape with an unexpected degree of clarity, which I documented at the time in a .

    But seeing a possibility and building it are very different things.

    For several years the MAP itself did not materially advance. Life circumstances required turning my attention elsewhere before the conditions finally aligned to begin the work in earnest.

    Only in mid-2021 was it finally possible to begin building the MAP. We are now approaching five years of focused development, and for the first time the system is nearing a point where it can begin to exist in the world in a very limited form—an early foundation upon which real experimentation can begin.

    This moment matters.

    Because the interesting part of this journey is not the technology itself. The interesting part is what people may eventually build on top of it.

    The path to this point required re-examining many of the assumptions underlying the digital architectures I had worked with for most of my career. Over time it became clear that many of the patterns embedded in our technological infrastructure were shaped by the same assumptions that gave rise to the systems now driving us into collapse. I had to unlearn architectural habits I had long taken for granted.

    These essays are my attempt to make that journey of reasoning transparent. They are not written as academic theses. While there is ample evidence behind many of the claims made, the purpose here is not exhaustive citation. Instead, my aim is to lay out the trajectory of thought that led me to believe that a different kind of coordination substrate may be necessary.

    Thus, these essays are not about the MAP itself. They are about a clear definition of the problems the MAP is being designed to help address.

    Part I – Civilizational Transitions Require New Systems¶

    We Are Living in Collapse¶

    In the previous essay, I argued that we are not approaching collapse — we are already living inside it. The evidence is ecological, political, psychological, and institutional. But collapse is not merely a convergence of crises. It is the predictable outcome of a worldview — a developmental stage of culture rooted in separation, domination, and extraction.

    That stage, which the Planet Project refers to as the Material Stage of culture, can be traced back thousands of years. It rests on a set of assumptions that once appeared workable: that humans stand apart from the living systems that sustain them; that life is a competitive struggle in which success requires domination; and that prosperity depends on ever-increasing extraction from the Earth.

    Those assumptions are now colliding with planetary reality. The logic underlying the Material Stage is exhausting itself from within.

    Yet as Barbara Marx Hubbard often reminded us, every evolutionary quantum leap has been preceded by crisis, chaos, and breakdown.

    Civilizational destabilization does not occur in a vacuum. As the assumptions of the Material Stage lose coherence, new cultural patterns are beginning to emerge.

    Across many domains—ecological restoration, regenerative agriculture, commons-based governance, relational worldviews, and partnership-oriented social systems—a different set of cultural assumptions is beginning to take shape.

    The Planet Project refers to this transformation as the Great Transition: a shift from a civilization organized around domination and extraction to one grounded in partnership, interdependence, and care for life.

    This transition is not guaranteed to succeed. History offers no assurance that emerging cultural patterns will stabilize into a new civilizational stage. But the tension between these two cultural logics—Material Culture and Planetary Culture—is already visible across the world.

    Understanding how such transitions unfold therefore becomes essential. Because cultural transformation does not occur through ideas alone. It must eventually take institutional form.

    “We are not just experiencing a breakdown — we are living through the early stages of a breakthrough.” *– *

    Why Cultural Transitions Require New Systems¶

    Civilizations reproduce themselves through systems that embody and reinforce the stories they believe about the world. Economic systems, governance systems, educational institutions, energy systems, food systems, and the technology substrate that amplifies them are not neutral infrastructures. They embody the assumptions of the culture that created them.

    A worldview rooted in competition builds competitive systems.

    A worldview rooted in domination builds centralized control.

    A worldview rooted in extraction builds growth-optimized machinery.

    Once established, those systems strengthen the worldview that produced them. They reward certain behaviors, normalize certain assumptions, and make alternative patterns of organization difficult to sustain.

    Anthropologist Marvin Harris proposed understanding this dynamic through three interacting structural layers of civilization.

    At the highest level are the cultural narratives and values through which societies interpret the world — the realm of worldview, ideology, and meaning.

    At the structural layer are the social and organizational arrangements through which societies coordinate behavior: governance systems, legal frameworks, and educational institutions.

    Beneath those systems lies the infrastructure — the material and technological foundations of civilization: economic systems, agriculture, energy, transportation networks, industrial production, and digital infrastructure.

    Harris, as a cultural materialist, argued that causal influence primarily flows upward from infrastructure. In his view, material conditions — ecological constraints, technologies, and economic systems — ultimately shape institutions and cultural beliefs.

    Others argue the opposite: that transformation begins with shifts in consciousness, and that new structures and infrastructures will naturally follow.

    My view is that both perspectives capture part of the truth.

    These layers co-evolve. Worldview shapes systems. Systems shape infrastructure. Infrastructure, once built, reinforces the worldview that produced it.

    Civilizations therefore do more than express cultural assumptions — they stabilize them.

    This has an important implication.

    If a life-affirming worldview is to stabilize, the systems built upon it must be consciously redesigned. Anything less, and the cultural transition cannot take root. The old systems inexorably pull culture back toward the patterns they were designed to reproduce.

    Technology Amplifies Civilizational Assumptions¶

    Technology then adds a second dynamic. Technological power increases the scale, speed, and reach with which a civilization’s systems operate. And in so doing, it amplifies the worldview assumptions which those systems embody.

    The structural and infrastructural layers stabilize a worldview. They keep it from drifting away from its core assumptions. The technology substrate extends and intensifies their effects.

    Technology is civilization’s acceleration layer.

    When those systems are built on assumptions of extraction and domination, technological power magnifies their consequences.

    Part II The Civilizational Design Window¶

    “Today the very extremity of our global crisis creates an extraordinary opportunity for creative transformation.” – Dr. Louis G. Herman

    The depth of the crises we face can easily lead to despair. When the scale of ecological breakdown, institutional failure, and social fragmentation becomes visible, grief is a natural response. Many people experience a profound sense of disillusionment as the stories that once explained the world lose their coherence.

    As noted in the previous blog, “Grief is not a sign of failure—it is a sign of integrity. To love this world is to mourn what is being lost, and to rise in defense of what can still be saved.”

    That grief is real. And it deserves to be honored.

    But if our attention stops there, we are seeing only half of the story.

    Without letting go of our grief, can we widen our gaze to also notice that as these stories begin to lose their believability and their hold, vast space opens for new stories to emerge?

    When foundational systems begin to fail, they force questions that would otherwise remain unasked. The possibility of redesign suddenly becomes not only conceivable, but unavoidable.

    This is why zooming out to see the larger arc of our evolution becomes so powerful. The central finding of is that the memetic roots —the hidden assumptions underlying the structural and infrastructural foundations of modern society—can be traced back not 50 or 500 years, but roughly 5,000 years! That is when the illusion of separation began tightening its grip on human culture. From that point forward, the archaeological record shows the rise of domination and exploitation.

    Pause for a moment to consider the profound implications of that.

    Virtually every system we rely on – economic, educational, governance, food, energy, transportation – arose in the 5,000 year stage of Material Culture that is now collapsing. Everything modern society has taught us was true, permanent, and ever-lasting is suddenly in question. As the bottom drops out of what we thought was real, the range of what may be possible suddenly expands. We are on the precipice of an opportunity for change at a depth and scale never before experienced in human history.

    I want to tread carefully here lest this sounds like I am indulging in some utopian fantasy. I am making a claim about the extraordinary size of the opportunity, not the ease of realizing it. The growth-addicted extractive, exploitative economy continues to consolidate and centralize its power, gobbling up both human creativity and the natural world. The perverse incentives and multi-polar traps of our civilizational systems have us careening towards a future that no one wants, but even the top 1% are powerless to avoid.

    Meanwhile, fewer than 15% of people even glimpse the collapse and probably less than 2% are aware we are already in its early stages. In other words, the vast majority of the world’s population are still engaged in the daily struggle for survival within a domination culture that is seen as “just the way things are… just the way the world works.”

    The depth of the crisis matters. Only when the stabilizing forces of the current systems begin to loosen their grip can new systems start to gain a foothold. Until recently, those forces have been so powerful that fundamental change to these systems has been unimaginable. But that is exactly what is now shifting.

    The very forces destabilizing our current systems are simultaneously opening space for entirely new ones.

    Even if only 1-2% of people are collapse aware, that is still on the order of 80-160 million people. And, indeed, millions of people are already exploring what it might mean to organize society around very different assumptions about life. These experiments are not coordinated through a single ideology or movement. They are emerging spontaneously as people respond to the same underlying realization: the systems we inherited are no longer capable of sustaining life on a finite planet.

    Across the world this process is already underway.

    In regenerative agriculture and ecological restoration.

    In commons-based governance and cooperative enterprise.

    In experiments with bioregional organization, mutual aid networks, and community-centered economies.

    A global inquiry has begun—a search for systems that express a different relationship with the living world.

    We are living, in other words, inside a rare civilizational design window. For a brief moment in history, the assumptions organizing human civilization are fluid enough that new possibilities can be explored.

    Design here does not mean that someone can draw up the blueprint for a future civilization. Complex living systems do not unfold according to a master plan. They evolve through countless acts of sensing, responding, experimenting, and learning. What can be shaped are the conditions within which those processes unfold — the cultural narratives, institutional patterns, and technological infrastructures that influence which possibilities are able to take root.

    The task before us is therefore not to engineer a finished system, but to help guide the emergence of new ones.

    And we still possess extraordinary resources for doing so. Never before has humanity had access to such vast scientific knowledge, technological capability, and global communication networks. Even as ecological systems are under strain, the biosphere still retains immense regenerative capacity. The material and energetic inheritance of the industrial age has created tools that can now be redirected toward very different purposes.

    But this window will not remain open indefinitely. As ecological degradation accelerates and institutional systems destabilize further, the capacity to intentionally reshape our civilizational infrastructure will shrink. The opportunity before us exists precisely because collapse has progressed far enough that the foundations of the current system are beginning to loosen — but not so far that humanity has lost the capacity to guide the emergence of new ones.

    We are living in the narrow interval between recognition and irreversible loss.

    As long as collapse remains unacknowledged, our collective energy is consumed defending (or fighting) the systems that are failing. Collapse awareness, painful as it may be, is the threshold that allows us to move beyond denial and ask the generative question that began this inquiry: What becomes possible when we stop denying collapse—and start building for what comes after?

    But recognition alone does not tell us how to move forward. Awareness of collapse can awaken responsibility, yet fear by itself cannot sustain the creativity required for civilizational transformation.

    “The most common way people give up their power is by thinking they don’t have any.” – Alice Walker

    When people experience themselves as powerless, imagination contracts. Possibility narrows. The future begins to feel fixed. Yet it is precisely this capacity for imagination that allows new possibilities to be seen, shared, and brought into being. In times of uncertainty, diverse creativity is not a luxury—it is the primary source of adaptation.

    What draws people into sustained collective action is not fear, but the felt possibility of creating something worth moving toward. Shared aspirations can organize behavior without centralized control, aligning individuals and communities through meaning rather than coercion. If we are able to successfully navigate the Great Transition, it will depend on the clarity and pull of a shared vision of what is possible.

    “One of the greatest tools of manipulation used by systems of power is the belief that our current socio-economic order is a reflection of reality itself.” –Open Civics

    These tools of social control would have us believe that the systems surrounding us are simply the way things are. Economic arrangements, governance structures, and technological infrastructures begin to feel as natural and inevitable as gravity.

    But they are not. They are human arrangements. And arrangements can change.

    Pause for a moment and imagine what that might mean.

    What if an economic system were designed not around separation, competition, domination, and extraction, but around caring and the recognition that life is fundamentally interconnected?

    What if the purpose of an economy were not endless growth, but the flourishing of living systems?

    What if success were measured by the degree to which a society created the conditions for life to thrive — human and nonhuman alike?

    What kinds of institutions would emerge?

    What kinds of technologies would serve such a civilization?

    What kinds of relationships would shape our collective lives?

    This civilizational design window demands every ounce of creativity—individual and collective—that we can muster. And discovering how to coordinate that collective creativity at scale without reproducing the meme of domination is one of its central challenges.

    Part III The Architecture of Consolidation¶

    The challenge becomes clearer when we look closely at how modern technological systems evolve in practice.

    Digital infrastructure is often imagined as inherently decentralizing and democratizing. Yet the history of enterprise computing over the past four decades tells a different story. Again and again, technological ecosystems that begin open and distributed gradually consolidate around a small number of dominant platforms.

    In the 1980s, most enterprise software existed as isolated point solutions. Organizations purchased separate systems for accounting, human resources, customer records, inventory management, and logistics. Each application solved a specific problem, but coordination between them required significant effort. Data had to be transferred manually or through custom integrations. Workflows frequently crossed system boundaries.

    As organizations adopted more digital tools, the burden of integration grew rapidly.

    Vendors began responding by expanding the scope of their offerings. Databases evolved into application platforms. Application platforms evolved into enterprise software suites. Suites eventually expanded into cloud ecosystems that bundled infrastructure, identity, analytics, collaboration, and application development within a single environment.

    Each stage reduced friction for the organizations adopting these systems. Integration became easier. Workflows became smoother. Data moved more easily across applications.

    But each stage also increased switching costs.

    As organizations accumulated data, processes, and institutional knowledge within these environments, leaving them became increasingly difficult. Data models, business logic, operational processes, and employee training all became entangled with the platform’s architecture.

    What began as a solution to integration friction gradually produced a different outcome: structural lock-in.

    Over time, ecosystems consolidated around the platforms that reduced friction most effectively. Those platforms accumulated users, data, and influence. Competitors found it increasingly difficult to enter the market, not necessarily because their technology was inferior, but because they could not match the breadth of functionality required to even be considered. And customers were increasingly locked-in because the cost of switching providers became prohibitively high.

    The result was growing power asymmetry between the operators of the infrastructure and the participants who relied upon it.

    This dynamic is not merely the result of corporate ambition or regulatory failure. It is structural — a predictable outcome of architectures optimized for integration, consolidation, and scale under the assumptions of the Material Stage. The result is a powerful reinforcing loop.

    Why This Matters Now

    The regenerative technology landscape today resembles the early stages of that earlier cycle. Most tools are still point solutions: platforms for community governance, ecological data, local currencies, collaboration, and knowledge sharing. As adoption grows, their users naturally begin asking for integration.

    This is a natural sign that the ecosystem is maturing. But this is also where early patterns get set that are difficult to correct later.

    If integration is achieved through the same architectural patterns that shaped enterprise computing and today’s digital platforms, the result will likely be the same: consolidation of functionality, rising lock-in, and growing asymmetry of power.

    And the stakes are far larger than improving coordination among a few thousand regenerative initiatives.

    The task we have set for ourselves in this series is to explore how civilizational systems might be reimagined so that they express and reinforce the memes of planetary culture at global scale.

    That ambition requires more than a collection of loosely connected applications. It requires a technological substrate capable of supporting the full spectrum of functions individuals and groups rely upon to participate in society: identity, communication, coordination, exchange, resource flows, governance, knowledge creation, learning, and collective sensemaking.

    If the platforms that mediate those functions follow historical patterns of development, they will concentrate visibility, influence, and control in the hands of infrastructure operators. The structural asymmetries of the Material Stage will persist regardless of the intentions of those building the tools.

    Power to the People

    Inverting that asymmetry requires something more demanding.

    It requires an architecture that allows the capabilities people need to function in modern society to interoperate seamlessly without enclosing participation inside proprietary platforms.

    In other words, the challenge is not merely to build new applications.

    It is to design a civilizational substrate capable of providing integration without enclosure, coordination without domination, and shared infrastructure without structural capture.

    The Architectural Question

    That challenge leads directly to the deeper design question this essay explores:

    What kind of technological substrate can provide seamless coordination and shared functionality without concentrating structural power in the entities operating the infrastructure?

    In other words:

    How do we invert the power asymmetry and achieve integration without enclosure?

    Part IV Conditions for a Life-Serving Substrate¶

    If systems express worldview — and reinforce it — then the emergence of a planetary culture requires infrastructures capable of stabilizing a different worldview.

    A life-serving technological substrate must avoid repeating the patterns and practices that have led to collapse. This is a very real risk since hidden assumptions have given rise to long-standing habits of behavior. This section articulates five conditions that a life-serving substrate must satisfy.

    Condition 1 — Keeping the Future Open¶

    A life-serving coordination substrate must remain fundamentally open-ended and generative — enabling experimentation, recombination, and the continual evolution of new forms of cooperation.

    The coordination systems of industrial civilization did not arise overnight. They evolved over centuries through experimentation, conflict, and adaptation. Markets, nation-states, legal systems, corporations, and bureaucracies are the accumulated result of thousands of years of institutional evolution.

    Planetary culture lacks that history.

    We are only beginning to explore what forms of coordination might allow billions of people and millions of communities to cooperate while preserving autonomy, diversity, and ecological integrity. This means that the design of planetary-scale coordination systems cannot rely solely on the imagination of their creators.

    The future cannot be fully specified in advance. Instead it must be discovered through practice — through experimentation across cultures, communities, and ecosystems. In this regard, the diversity of human creativity is not an obstacle to coherence. It is the primary source of discovery.

    Biological evolution offers a useful analogy. All life on Earth emerges from a remarkably small set of molecular components. The four nucleotides that make up DNA combine through simple generative rules to produce the extraordinary diversity of living organisms. Complexity arises not from complex primitives, but from generative recombination.

    Civilizational systems require a similar principle. If the coordination infrastructure of planetary culture attempts to impose rigid ontologies or predetermined schemas, it risks constraining the very creativity through which new forms of cooperation must emerge. The challenge, therefore, is to create infrastructures that encourage diversity of expression while still allowing coherence to arise.

    Such systems must support experimentation, recombination, and adaptation. They must enable participants to extend the structures through which meaning, value, and relationships are represented.

    Condition 2 — Scaling Coherence Without Domination¶

    A life-serving coordination substrate must support self-organizing coherence at scale — enabling people and communities to align voluntarily around purposes that sustain and regenerate life, without domination, centralized control, or reduction to price signals.

    Planetary-scale crises require coordination at planetary scale.

    Yet historically, large-scale coordination has relied on hierarchical power structures, enforcement, and centralized authority. Nation states, corporations, bureaucracies, and platforms solve the scaling problem through command structures and incentive manipulation.

    But domination suppresses diversity and erodes autonomy.

    Planetary culture requires something different: coherence without coercion.

    Some would argue that markets already solve this problem. And it is true that markets allow large numbers of participants to coordinate their behavior without centralized planning, using price signals to guide exchange.

    But markets coordinate only a narrow band of human activity — those interactions that can be expressed through price. And then market systems tend to reshape coordination itself so that what cannot be priced becomes increasingly difficult to coordinate at scale. Diverse forms of value are gradually compressed into a single signal: financial return.

    Activities that increase profit are amplified, scaled, and rewarded. Activities whose value cannot easily be translated into price — ecological restoration, care, cultural renewal, community resilience — often remain structurally invisible. In economic language, they are treated as externalities: real effects on the living world that fall outside the accounting systems guiding economic decision-making. Regenerative work is not just undervalued — it is systematically excluded from the signal system that coordinates economic behavior.

    This raises an even deeper question. Markets do coordinate action — but to what end?

    In modern economies the answer is clear: toward the maximization of financial return. And this inexhaustible, unceasing, pursuit of financial return is exactly the dynamic driving collapse.

    The naive dogma that competitive pursuit of financial return will somehow, as a side-effect, lead to conditions that serve life as a side-effect is failing us badly.

    The purpose matters. If we want conditions that serve life, that should be our purpose.

    Condition 3 — Making Life-Serving Work Economically Viable¶

    A life-serving coordination system must enable multi-capital value flows capable of sustaining regenerative work directly.

    The Livelihood Paradox

    Today, much of the work people feel called to do exists inside a structural contradiction.

    Our hearts pull us toward work that nourishes life—care, creativity, restoration, community, meaning. Yet our economic system fails to recognize or reward this work. The result is felt as a tension between what I have come to refer to as our Essential Self and our Economic Self.

    Have you felt this tension?

    Have you ever experienced the need to take a “day job” in order to provide enough financial support to allow you to do the work you really want to be doing?

    Individuals often maintain livelihoods inside systems they know are contributing to collapse in order to fund the work they believe might help heal it.

    For years I compartmentalized this split – living two parallel lives. In 2017, I wrote about this tension (and its reconciliation) in . And in that article I also noted that we can see this same underlying tension at multiple levels of society.

    “For-profit enterprises are allowed to externalize so many environmental and social costs onto society as a whole, that NGO's are springing up at an unprecedented pace to minister to all of this externalized pain. But NGO's are still dependent upon the engine of commerce for their trickle-down donations. And, to a certain extent, the split between the for-profit ("economic self") and non-profit ("essential self") sectors, just gives greater license to corporations to continue toxic business practices and an ever-narrower focus on profit.”

    This tension feels irreconcilable and inevitable.

    And the fact is, this tension IS irreconcilable within the logic of our current economic system. Modern economies are organized around a clear central incentive: the maximization of financial return. Activities that increase profit are amplified, scaled, and rewarded. The value of being in service to life is neither recognized nor rewarded.

    But incentives are not laws of nature. What today appears irreconcilable may simply be the predictable outcome of the rules of the game we are currently playing.

    Change the rules, and different patterns of work, value, and livelihood become possible.

    This raises a practical question. What kind of economic architecture would allow work that nourishes life to also sustain the people doing it?

    The good news is that across the world, innovators have been experimenting for decades with new approaches: regenerative economics, commons-based governance, cooperative enterprise, solidarity economies, bioregional finance, and many others. Each of these efforts represents an attempt to realign human livelihood with the conditions that sustain life.

    From this range of exploration, one thing becomes clear: different communities are testing different ways of defining incentives, recognizing value, and organizing exchange.

    The deeper question, therefore, may not be which economic system will ultimately prevail, but what kinds of coordination infrastructures could support the breadth of experimentation our historical moment demands.

    Today’s dominant economic systems recognize only a narrow band of value — financial return. Yet the work that sustains living systems generates many forms of value: ecological restoration, strengthened relationships, cultural renewal, knowledge, resilience, and care.

    When these forms of value cannot be recognized or circulated within the systems that support livelihoods, they remain economically invisible. Work that nourishes life may generate immense value, yet still fail to sustain the people doing it.

    This structural invisibility lies at the heart of the Livelihood Paradox.

    What may be required, therefore, is not a single predefined economic model, but infrastructures capable of allowing diverse forms of value to be recognized, expressed, and circulated across networks of trust, contribution, and reciprocal exchange.

    Such infrastructures would not dictate the shape of future economies. Rather, they would provide the conditions within which different approaches to incentives, value, and exchange can be explored in practice.

    Only through such experimentation can the long-standing tension between what we might call the Essential Self and the Economic Self begin to dissolve.

    Regeneration must be able to support livelihoods directly rather than existing as a side project funded by the surplus of extraction.

    The remaining conditions arise from a deeper architectural pattern in modern digital infrastructure — one that concentrates power, fragments information, and encloses participation within platform boundaries.

    Condition 4 — Sovereignty Over Digital Life¶

    A life-serving coordination substrate must restore sovereignty over identity, data, and experience — returning stewardship of digital life to the individuals and communities who generate it.

    Modern digital infrastructure systematically concentrates control over information. Identity and content are stored inside provider-controlled systems. Even when individuals generate the data, the systems that host it determine how it can be accessed, shared, or used.

    Consider something familiar. What is the first thing you are asked for when you visit a new healthcare provider?

    Your complete medical history – from memory. The quality of your diagnosis and treatment may depend upon the degree to which you can recall (and are willing to share) the sensitive details of your entire medical history.

    Once you hand in the form, what happens to it?

    You have just handed sovereign control of your data to the healthcare provider and their medical information system.

    Do you ever see it again? Do you have any control over who they share it with or any visibility over who they HAVE shared it with? Even when some controls exist (e.g., HIPAA), you are still left wondering if they have the will and means to protect it from unauthorized access.

    Seeing a new doctor? Repeat the entire process… from memory.

    Now notice how this same ceding of control is repeated across virtually every digital application you use – from social media profiles to loan applications, from Google docs to online order history, from credit card information to AI Chats.

    Our personal data is fragmented across and hostaged by application providers.

    This diagram illustrates the internal structure of a typical digital application.

    Identity, data, logic, and experience are integrated within the boundaries of the application itself. To participate, individuals must establish identity within the platform and interact through the interface it provides.

    Each application therefore becomes a self-contained environment in which participation occurs. If I want the functionality the application provides, I must agree to have my data stored in their (the app provider’s) cloud, structured according to how they have decided to model that data. My access to that application is only via the User Interface they provide.

    When this architecture is replicated across dozens of services, the consequences become visible.

    When many vertically integrated applications surround an individual, identity, information, and attention fragment across platform boundaries.The result is a fragmented digital self:

    fragmented data

    fragmented identity

    fragmented experience

    fragmented notifications

    Participants must maintain multiple identities, manage separate data silos, and navigate dozens of notification streams competing for attention.

    The burden of bringing coherence across these fragments falls on the me. Furthermore, the application providers are actually working against my coherence. Their attention-economy (and, more recently, attachment-economy) business models have them using sophisticated AI-fueled cognitive techniques to keep me engaged in their fragment.

    The result is a systematic loss of sovereignty over both information and digital experience.

    Condition 5 — Coordination Without Concentrated Power¶

    A planetary substrate must enable efficient coordination without concentrating structural power in the entities operating the infrastructure.

    As we saw in the Architecture of Consolidation, market pressures drive solution providers to add more features.

    Zoom began as a video conferencing tool. Today it includes chat, persistent team messaging, whiteboards, document collaboration, scheduling, email, and AI assistants.

    Slack began as a messaging platform. It now includes huddles (video/audio meetings), document collaboration, workflow automation, whiteboards, and AI assistants.

    Miro began as a collaborative whiteboard. It now includes video meetings, chat, task management, document collaboration, and AI tools.

    Each platform begins as a point solution, but gradually adds features that overlap with the others.

    Did Zoom add chat because it believes it can do it dramatically better than Slack? Probably not. Platforms expand their feature sets because customers prefer meeting more of their needs within a single tool. That convenience is real. It saves teams from constantly jumping between applications with different logins, different group structures, and different user interfaces.

    But that convenience comes at a price.

    The Hidden Inefficiency of Platform Dominance

    At first glance, centralized platforms appear efficient. But at the ecosystem level they often produce the opposite. Duplicating features to keep users from jumping outside your product wastes creative energy. Resources that could be directed toward innovation are instead spent rebuilding the same foundational components over and over again.

    A truly generative digital ecosystem would allow these common functions to exist as shared infrastructure rather than proprietary silos.

    Open Source is a Partial Solution

    Open-source software partially addresses this problem by allowing shared components to be freely available. But open source alone does not solve the integration problem. Platforms succeed not only because of ownership, but because they provide coherent integrated experiences. Identity, communication, transactions, and coordination all happen within one environment.

    Open source code is available to predator and prey alike. Licensing mechanisms can protect code, but they cannot prevent the capture of the coordination infrastructure at a higher layer.

    Social Media Platforms make heavy use of open-source components (Linux, MySQL, TensorFlow, Kafka). Yet the platform still captures the value because it controls:

    Identity

    Data

    network relationships

    distribution algorithms

    The code is open, but the coordination layer is proprietary.

    The challenge is therefore deeper than open source versus proprietary software.

    Concentrating Power

    Accumulating more features reduces the likelihood that users will leave the platform to get their needs met. It’s a strategy of capture.

    Across providers, it creates barriers to entry. Newer — and often smaller — application developers may have a brilliant approach to a single capability. But if their product cannot cover a spectrum of features comparable to existing platforms, it rarely receives serious consideration.

    Across customers, it creates high switching costs. When I was at Sun Microsystems in the early 2000s, we estimated that switching from Oracle’s eBusiness Suite to SAP’s Business Suite would cost roughly $1 billion. The process required complex data transformations, re-engineering business processes, closing feature gaps, and retraining large portions of the workforce.

    The result is growing power asymmetry and structural lock-in. Over time, data, workflows, identity, and collaboration patterns accumulate inside the platform. Leaving becomes increasingly difficult — not because the platform is perfect, but because the cost of exit becomes prohibitive.

    Power flows to the platform.

    This pattern is not merely the result of corporate ambition. It arises from a structural dynamic often called integration gravity: integrated systems reduce friction, reduced friction improves efficiency, and efficiency wins under competitive pressure.

    The deeper challenge is not only the emergence of dominant platforms, but the difficulty of inverting the resulting asymmetry once it forms.

    As artificial intelligence becomes more capable, this asymmetry grows. Platforms gain increasing ability to observe, predict, and influence behavior, while participants often lack visibility into how their information is used.

    Part V: A Civilization That Serves Life¶

    Across these two essays we began with a recognition that may be difficult to face but increasingly hard to ignore: we are already living through the early stages of civilizational collapse.

    The first essay explored how the converging disruptions of our time — ecological overshoot, social fragmentation, institutional breakdown, and a crisis of meaning — are not isolated problems but cascading expressions of a deeper systemic failure. The dominant worldview of modern civilization, and the systems built upon it, are no longer capable of sustaining the conditions for life, dignity, and meaning.

    Yet collapse awareness, painful as it may be, opens a deeper question:

    What becomes possible when we stop denying collapse — and begin building for what comes after?

    This second essay explored one implication of that question.

    Worldviews shape systems. Systems reinforce worldviews. And the infrastructures through which civilization operates — including technological infrastructure — stabilize and amplify the dynamics those systems embody.

    **If a new civilization is to take root, the systems that stabilize and amplify worldview must evolve as well. **

    Economic systems, governance systems, and cultural institutions must be reimagined so they reinforce the conditions for life rather than undermine them.

    But in a world where so much coordination now flows through digital infrastructure, these systems are increasingly shaped by the technological substrate through which they operate.

    A life-affirming worldview cannot take root without a fundamentally different substrate.

    A life-serving technological substrate must be capable of doing several difficult things at once. It must allow regenerative work to sustain itself economically. It must enable planetary-scale coordination without domination. It must restore informational sovereignty to the people and communities who generate the information that shapes their lives. It must prevent structural concentrations of power. And it must preserve the freedom for individuals and communities to shape how they interact with the systems that coordinate their shared activity.

    Any system that fails to meet these conditions will tend to reproduce the dynamics of the civilization now breaking down, regardless of the intentions behind it.

    These design principles did not emerge in the abstract. They grew out of decades of work in technology combined with a sustained inquiry into the deeper systemic causes of the metacrisis. Taken together, they point toward a simple conclusion.

    The architecture of coordination itself must change.

    The Memetic Activation Platform (MAP) emerged from that realization. It represents one attempt — still evolving — to explore what a technological substrate might look like if it were designed from the outset around life-serving principles: agent sovereignty, membrane-based information sharing, trust-mediated coordination, and multidimensional value flows capable of sustaining regenerative work.

    MAP is not intended as another platform competing for attention in an already crowded technological landscape. Its purpose is more foundational: to provide an infrastructural layer within which many different communities, applications, and experiments can coexist while preserving sovereignty, interoperability, and trust. In that sense, it is less an application than an attempt to create a different kind of container for technological collaboration.

    But technology alone will never be the heart of this transition.

    The deeper impulse driving this work lies in something far more fundamental: the recognition that we are living within an extraordinary civilizational design window. The very depth of the crises we face has exposed the assumptions underlying the systems that produced them. And in doing so, it has opened a space — brief, fragile, and unprecedented — in which the foundations of civilization itself can be reconsidered.

    Across the world, people are already responding to that opening in countless ways: restoring ecosystems, rebuilding community, experimenting with new forms of governance and cooperation, and rediscovering ways of living that honor our relationship with the living Earth.

    Across disciplines and traditions, thinkers are reaching for language to describe the civilizational transition now beginning to take shape.

    Thomas Berry spoke of the Ecozoic, an era in which humans live in mutually enhancing relationship with the Earth community. Glenn Albrecht describes the Symbiocene, defined by symbiosis between human systems and the living world. Planetary physicist Jude Currivan speaks of the emergence of a unitive worldview grounded in the fundamental interconnectedness of the cosmos. Riane Eisler describes the possibility of a partnership civilization, organized around care, reciprocity, and the flourishing of life. Systems thinker Louis Herman points toward the emergence of a planetary phase of civilization aligned with ecological responsibility and global cooperation. Others speak of Ecological Civilization, Regenerative Civilization, or what Martin Luther King Jr. called the Beloved Community. Joanna Macy writes of The Great Turning toward a life-sustaining society. Charles Eisenstein describes the more beautiful world our hearts know is possible.

    The language differs. But the direction is strikingly consistent.

    All point toward the possibility of a civilization organized not around domination and extraction, but around the flourishing of life.

    A civilization that serves life.

    The work of building such a civilization will unfold across many domains — cultural, ecological, social, spiritual, and technological. No single system, platform, or community will define it. But the infrastructures we build today will help shape the possibilities available to those who come after us.

    Which means the guiding question cannot simply be whether a technology works, or whether it scales, or whether it wins in the marketplace.

    The deeper question is far simpler.

    Does it help life thrive?

    Technology will inevitably amplify the memetic foundations embedded within it. We are not simply inheriting the systems that shape our world. We are choosing the substrate from which the next civilization will grow. Because in the end, the purpose of technology is not to advance itself.

    The purpose of technology — and of the systems through which civilization organizes itself — must be to serve life.
